The centre

What you will find in Sector 115

A room with light in it, shoes at the door, and people who will not ask you why you came.

The centre keeps a simple rhythm. It opens before dawn for the quietest meditation of the day, holds a study class each morning, and opens again in the evening for anyone finishing work. Between those hours it is used for courses, for conversations, and by people who simply want somewhere to sit.

Most of what happens here happens because someone local offered to make it happen. The teachers are not paid. The building is kept by the people who use it. When a school or a hospital in Noida asks for a session on stress or values, someone from this centre goes — and no invoice follows.

You do not need to be a Brahma Kumari to be welcome here. A large number of the people who come each week study nothing, join nothing, and simply sit in the evening meditation because it helps.

The organisation

The Brahma Kumaris began in 1937 in Hyderabad, Sindh, when a successful diamond merchant named Dada Lekhraj gave away his business and placed the whole of it in the hands of a committee of young women.

That decision — in that decade, in that place — is still the most striking fact about the organisation. Nearly ninety years later it remains led by women, and its administrative headquarters at Mount Abu in Rajasthan is one of the largest spiritual campuses in the world.

Today there are more than eight thousand five hundred centres in over a hundred and ten countries. The Brahma Kumaris hold general consultative status with the Economic and Social Council of the United Nations and consultative status with UNICEF. Every course taught at every one of those centres is free.

Students follow a simple discipline: a vegetarian diet without onion and garlic, no alcohol or tobacco, celibacy, and the early-morning meditation known as Amrit Vela. None of it is asked of a visitor.
